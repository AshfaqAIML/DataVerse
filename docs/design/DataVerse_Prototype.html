Sorting, the honest way

14 min 3 outcomes2 new terms ✓ 80% to pass checkpoint
Why this matters

Your sales team asks for "the top 5 months by revenue." Sorting sounds trivial — until ties and text months quietly reorder your rows and your conclusion. Analysts get burned by sorting weekly. Today you stop being one of them.

Sorting a table means putting rows in a chosen order — smallest first, biggest first, or alphabetical. pandas gives you one method for all of it: sort_values(). The honest part is what it does when two rows tie.

When values are equal, pandas keeps rows in their original order. That promise is called a stable sort. It sounds like a technicality; it's actually the difference between "August before July, as they appeared" and "July before August, alphabetically."

Try it — fair-share beam Interactive

Drag the big sale and watch which average moves. The mean chases extreme values; the median holds its ground.

Drag past 20 — the mean follows, the median doesn't budge.
Worked example — the tie that mattered
1

The question. "Order our drinks by monthly units sold, biggest first."

2

The attempt. We sort by units — but Latte and Mocha both sold 212 this month.

3

The wrong turn. A second sort, by name, "to be tidy." Now the tied rows are alphabetical — and no longer in week order. A quiet rewrite of history.

4

The fix. One sort, both keys: sort_values(["units", "week"]). Ties break by week — deliberately, in writing, where you can defend it.

Guided exercise attempt 1 of ∞ · hints are free

Sales wants "top 5 months by revenue, ties broken by month order." Which sort is honest?

ASort by revenue only — pandas ties keep month order automatically, because its sort is stable.
BSort by revenue, then sort again by month name — two passes is always safer.
CSort by month name only — months mostly line up with revenue anyway.
Exactly. One sort by revenue is enough — stability guarantees tied months keep their original order. Two passes (B) silently rewrites ties; C answers a different question.
Not this one. You picked the two-pass approach — it feels safer, but the second sort replaces the tie order instead of preserving it. Try hint 1 below, then look at option A again.
Hint ladder — reveals never spoil, Ada elaborates
What does pandas promise to do with rows that tie — reorder them, or leave them be? Re-read the second paragraph of this lesson.
Rule out any plan with a second sort pass. Then ask: for the ties that remain, is anything left to fix?
Parallel problem: "top students by score, ties by enrollment order." Solution: df.sort_values("score") — done. Enrollment order survives because the sort is stable. Your problem has the same shape.
In this lesson
  • Sorting reorders rows by one or more columns — sort_values().
  • Ties keep their original order: that's a stable sort, and it protects your conclusions.
  • Need a specific tie order? Add a second key on purpose: sort_values(["units", "week"]).
  • Sorting changes row order, never your data — the original table stays recoverable.

Your table has one row per sale, with a price column. Marketing wants "the 5 priciest sales, and for equal prices the earliest first." Which single call is honest?
